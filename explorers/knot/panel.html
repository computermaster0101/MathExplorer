When are two tangles the same?

A mathematical knot is a loop with its ends joined, so it can't be untied. Two knots are the same if one can be wiggled into the other without cutting. The crossing number is the fewest crossings any drawing of it can have: 3 for the trefoil, 4 for the figure-eight. The unknotting number is how many crossings you must swap to undo it.

Riding a knot

Press G and you travel along the knot. From on top you see the other strands sweep over and under you at every crossing: that over-and-under pattern is what makes it a knot. Ride round the trefoil and you pass under, over, under, over… and arrive back where you started without ever coming to an end.

Links and linking numbers

Several loops tangled together make a link. Count how many times one loop winds round another: that's the linking number. Two rings of a chain have linking number 1. But the number doesn't tell the whole story: the Whitehead link has linking number 0 and still can't be pulled apart, and the Borromean rings fall apart if you cut any one of them.

Knots on a doughnut

Wind a string p times the long way round a doughnut and q times the short way and you get the torus knot (p, q). When p and q share no factor it's one knot; when they do, it falls into several linked loops. The trefoil is (3, 2) and the cinquefoil (5, 2).

Granny and square

Tie two trefoils one after the other. If both twist the same way you get the granny knot; if one is the mirror image of the other you get the square (reef) knot. They are made of the same pieces and have the same Alexander polynomial, yet no wiggling turns one into the other. Sailors know the difference: the granny slips.
