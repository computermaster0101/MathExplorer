The Kelvin problem

How do you divide space into bubbles that all hold the same amount, using the least wall? Kelvin guessed truncated octahedra in 1887. In 1993 Weaire and Phelan beat him by 0.3% with two kinds of bubble. Nobody knows whether anything does better still. The Numbers group shows each foam's wall score: the surface of a bubble compared with its volume, scaled so the size doesn't matter. A sphere would score 4.84, but spheres can't fill space. Kelvin's foam scores 5.315, Weaire–Phelan 5.295 (with flat films; bending them saves a little more).

Plateau's laws

In 1873 the Belgian physicist Joseph Plateau, by then blind, worked out from his experiments the rules every soap foam obeys: films meet three at a time along each edge, at 120°, and edges meet four at a time at each corner, at about 109.5° (the angle at the middle of a tetrahedron). Jean Taylor proved in 1976 that no other meetings are stable. Cubes and rhombic dodecahedra break these rules, so a real foam never forms them.

Frank–Kasper foams

Every bubble in these foams has 12, 14, 15 or 16 faces, all of them pentagons and hexagons, with the hexagons never touching. Charles Frank and John Kasper found this family in 1958 in the crystals of metal alloys. Later the same structures turned up in soap-like molecules, in polymers and in gas hydrates. Weaire–Phelan is one of them. Colour by Faces and use Only bubbles with to see each kind on its own, or Pull apart to separate them.

Real foams

Real foams are random and uneven. A bubble in a three-dimensional foam has about 13.5 faces on average, and pentagons are the commonest face. Over time gas seeps through the films from small bubbles (higher pressure inside) to big ones, so the small shrink and vanish and the foam coarsens. Settle evens out a random foam step by step.

Bubbles in a tube

Blow bubbles into a tube and they pack in a few neat ways, chosen by how big they are compared with the tube: in a row (bamboo), alternating sides (zigzag), twisting, or in spirals of three or more. The same patterns turn up when spheres are packed in cylinders, from ball bearings to the cells of some plant stems. Change Bubble length to see where each one stops looking right.

Flat foams and the double bubble

Between two plates a foam is flat. Equal bubbles make a honeycomb, which Thomas Hales proved in 1999 uses the least wall. An uneven flat foam coarsens by von Neumann's rule: a bubble with more than six sides grows, one with fewer shrinks, one with exactly six stays the same. And two bubbles stuck together always make three spherical films meeting at 120°. That this double bubble uses the least film was proved only in 2000, after a century of trying.
