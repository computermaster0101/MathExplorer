The corners decide the world

Put q tiles with p sides round every corner. Each corner of a flat p-sided tile is 180 − 360 ÷ p degrees. If q of them add up to exactly 360, the tiles lie flat: that only happens for squares (4 round a corner), triangles (6) and hexagons (3). If they add up to less, the tiles fold up into a ball: the five Platonic solids. If they add up to more, there's too much tile to lie flat: it ruffles into the hyperbolic plane, which never runs out of room.

The hyperbolic disc

Henri Poincaré's map squeezes the whole infinite hyperbolic plane into a disc. Every tile here is really the same size; they only look smaller near the rim, which is infinitely far away. Straight lines are drawn as arcs that meet the rim at right angles. M. C. Escher used this map for his Circle Limit prints. Press G to glide and you're walking through it: the tiles keep coming, and there are always more.

Mirrors make the whole pattern

Turn on The mirror lines: every tile is cut into 2p identical triangles, each the mirror image of its neighbours. One triangle, reflected over and over in its three sides, makes the whole tiling. That's exactly how this picture is drawn: each point on screen is reflected back into the first triangle, counting how many mirrors it crossed.

One triangle, seven tilings

Willem Abraham Wythoff noticed in 1918 that the mirror triangle makes more than the regular tiling. Put the corner of the tiles somewhere else in the triangle, at the same distance from some of its mirrors, and reflect: the edges from that point make new tilings with two or three kinds of tile, all with every corner alike. From the dodecahedron's triangle come the icosidodecahedron, the rhombicosidodecahedron and the truncated icosahedron, which is the pattern on a football.

Dual tilings

Put a dot in every tile and join the dots of tiles that share an edge. You get another tiling, the dual, with p and q swapped: hexagons give triangles, the cube gives the octahedron. Turn on The dual tiling to see both at once.

Seventeen ways to repeat

Wallpaper, tiled floors and fabric repeat in two directions. Besides sliding, a pattern can have turns (by a half, a third, a quarter or a sixth), mirrors, and glides (a mirror and a slide at once). There are exactly 17 ways to combine them, no more. The motif here has no symmetry of its own, so you can see each copy turned or mirrored.

Patterns that never repeat

In 1974 Roger Penrose found two tile shapes that can cover the whole plane, but never in a repeating pattern: slide a copy of it any distance and it never fits back on itself. Yet it's far from random. It has five-fold symmetry (impossible for any repeating pattern), and the count of the two tiles tends to the golden ratio, 1.618…. In 1982 Dan Shechtman found crystals arranged this way, quasicrystals, and won a Nobel Prize for it.
