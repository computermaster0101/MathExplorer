One side or two?

A sheet of paper has two sides. Glue a strip into a loop with a half twist and it has only one: run a finger along the middle and you come back on the "other" side without crossing an edge. Surfaces like this are called non-orientable. Tick Colour the two sides differently and follow the colour round: on a one-sided surface it swaps where the surface joins up, because there was never a second side.

Counting a surface: the Euler number

Cover any closed surface with polygons and work out corners − edges + faces. The answer doesn't depend on how you cut it up: it is 2 for a sphere, 0 for a torus or a Klein bottle, 1 for the projective plane (Boy's surface), and drops by 2 for every extra handle. Together with "one side or two", this number tells every closed surface apart.

Cutting a Möbius strip down the middle

Cut a plain loop down the middle and you get two loops. Cut a Möbius strip down the middle and you get one loop, twice as long, with two full twists. Cut a strip with two half twists and you get two loops linked together. Choose Twisted band, set the half twists, and press Cut down the middle (or N) to make the cut.

Soap films and minimal surfaces

A soap film pulls itself as tight as it can, so it settles into the surface with the least area for its frame. At every point it curves up one way exactly as much as it curves down the other, so its mean curvature is zero. These are minimal surfaces. Some go on forever in every direction, like the gyroid: you can fly through it in Labyrinths.

Walking round a Möbius strip

Press G and you stand on the surface. Keep going the long way round (T). On a plain loop you come back on the same side. On a Möbius strip you come back upside down, on the "other" side, without ever crossing an edge; go round again and you're back where you started. The colours show which side the formula calls the front: on a one-sided surface it changes under your feet.

Curvature: bowls, saddles and horns

A sphere curves the same way in every direction, like a bowl. A saddle curves up one way and down the other. Gauss found that this curvature can be measured by someone living on the surface, without ever leaving it, by drawing triangles: on a bowl their angles add up to more than 180°, on a saddle to less. The pseudosphere is a saddle everywhere, by the same amount.
