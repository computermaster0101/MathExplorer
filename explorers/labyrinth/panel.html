Two worlds in one space

An endless labyrinth like the gyroid is one surface that splits all of space into two separate tunnel systems. They wind round each other forever but never meet: to get from one to the other you must go through the wall. Fly through one (G), then press N to step through the wall into the other. Try the beacon game: some beacons are in the other maze.

The best way to fill space with bubbles

Soap films meet in threes along every edge, at 120°, and edges meet in fours at every corner: Joseph Plateau's laws (1873). Kelvin's foam obeys them with one shape of bubble; Weaire and Phelan found a mix of two shapes that uses less film. Is there a better one still? Nobody knows.

A maze is a tree

Draw a dot in every room and a line through every doorway. In a "perfect" maze there's exactly one route between any two rooms, so the picture is a tree: it reaches everywhere and never loops back. How the tree is grown gives the maze its character. Wandering makes long winding corridors; growing outward makes lots of short dead ends; random walks pick perfectly at random among all possible mazes.

The shortest way out

The computer finds the shortest route by spreading out from where you stand, one step at a time in every direction at once, until it reaches the exit (breadth-first search). Press G to watch it drive that route. Then drive it yourself: your score is the shortest route divided by the route you took, so 100% means perfect.

Keep your hand on the wall

In a maze on one floor with no loops, keeping one hand on the wall always gets you out. Add loops and put the goal in the middle, and your hand can lead you round an island forever. In 3D there's no "left wall" to follow at all. Charles Trémaux's 19th-century method works in any maze: mark each passage as you go, and never take a passage marked twice. The breadcrumbs here are his marks.

Labyrinth or maze?

A maze has choices and dead ends. A labyrinth, in the old sense, has none: one path winds back and forth and must reach the centre. The Cretan labyrinth on ancient coins visits its seven rings in the order 3, 2, 1, 4, 7, 6, 5. Count the possible orders that never cross and you get 1, 1, 2, 3, 8, 14, 42, 81, 262… the "open meander" numbers: there are 42 different seven-ring labyrinths.
