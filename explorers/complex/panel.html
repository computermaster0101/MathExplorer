Reading the colours

Every point z of the plane is a complex number. The function sends it somewhere else, to w = f(z). The colour at z shows where w points: its angle sets the colour (red to the right, then round the rainbow), and with rings on, each ring is one doubling of its size. So the picture shows the whole function at once, every input with its output.

Zeros and poles

Where every colour meets at one point, the function is zero (all angles are possible next to 0) or infinite. Go round a zero and the colours run one way round the rainbow; round a pole they run the other way. Count the trips round the rainbow and you know how many of each are inside: the argument principle.

Angles kept: conformal maps

Choose Grid: the lines show where the real and imaginary parts of w are whole numbers. Wherever the function is smooth they still cross at right angles, however bent they are. Complex functions keep angles (they are conformal), which is why they're used to draw maps, design aircraft wings (the Joukowski map) and solve flow problems.

The Riemann sphere

Wrap the whole plane round a ball, with 0 at the bottom and infinity at the top. Then a pole is just another point, and 1 ÷ z simply turns the ball upside down. Switch to The Riemann sphere to see all of a function at once, infinity included.

Famous functions

Some functions are so useful they have names. The Gamma function carries factorials (1 × 2 × 3 × …) to every complex number. Riemann's zeta function encodes the prime numbers; whether all its interesting zeros lie on one straight line is the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics. Weierstrass's ℘ repeats in two directions at once, like wallpaper.

From one step to a fractal

Apply z² + c once and you get two smooth swirls. Feed the answer back in again and again (raise Steps) and the swirls fold into finer and finer detail: a Julia set appears. Fractals are complex functions applied over and over. Open in Fractals takes the same function across.
