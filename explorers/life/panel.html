Three rules, endless life

In 1970 John Conway invented a game with no players. Each square is alive or dead. Every step, a dead square with exactly three live neighbours comes alive, a live one with two or three survives, and all the rest die. That's all, yet out of it come gliders that crawl across the grid, guns that fire them forever, and even working computers built from glider streams.

Change the rule, change the world

The rule is written B (how many neighbours make a birth) / S (how many let a cell survive). Conway's is B3/S23. HighLife adds birth on six and grows replicators; Day & Night is symmetric, so dead and alive behave alike; Maze grows corridors. Type your own and see what kind of world it makes.

Smooth life: Lenia

Bert Chan's Lenia (2018) makes everything smooth: cells hold any amount between 0 and 1, they feel a soft ring of neighbours rather than eight squares, and they change a little at a time. With the right numbers and a carefully shaped start, creatures appear that hold their shape and glide, looking eerily like microscope life. From a random smear, as here, you more often see a world grow: coral-like tangles or fingerprint stripes spreading out from each patch. Try the two sliders: small changes decide whether everything dies, holds or takes over.

How the leopard got its spots

In 1952 Alan Turing asked how a smooth embryo could grow a pattern. His answer: two chemicals that spread at different speeds, one making more of itself and the other using it up. Here a feed brings in fresh chemical and a kill takes the other away. Small changes to those two numbers give spots, stripes, mazes or dividing cells, like the markings on fish, shells and big cats.
