What you're seeing

What you're seeing. A 4D shape can't fit in our space, so we look at its 3D "shadow", the way a globe gets flattened into a map.

Perspective is like a camera sitting out along the 4th axis: corners nearer it (high W) look bigger, which gives the classic cube inside a cube. Orthographic just drops the W coordinate, like a shadow under the midday sun. Stereographic wraps the shape onto a 4D sphere and flattens it like a world map.

Straight vs rounded. Straight joins the projected corners with bars, which print with fewer overhangs. Rounded lets each edge follow its arc around the 4D sphere. Rounded + stereographic is the most faithful view, since every cell comes out as a curved bubble. Thicker tubes are nearer along W.

Rotating in XW / YW / ZW turns the shape through the 4th axis. Cubes look like they turn inside out because they pass "through" each other, the way a 3D cube's shadow seems to flip on a wall. 4D rainbow gives each dimension its own colour channel: X controls red, Y green, Z blue, and W lightness (pale toward W+, deep toward W−). Painted on colours the shape itself, as if you'd painted it before spinning, so you can watch the inner parts travel to the outside and back. 4D depth colours by where each point is right now along W, like a heat map. Edge axis colours each edge by which of the four axes it mostly runs along, so parallel edges share a colour.

Counting check: Euler's rule

Count a polyhedron's corners (V), edges (E) and faces (F) and you always get V − E + F = 2, for any shape without holes. One dimension up, add the cells (C) and the rule becomes V − E + F − C = 0.

Open this on a shape to check it live.

The pattern keeps alternating: in every odd dimension the alternating sum is 2, in every even dimension it's 0. The n-D shapes show it too, using formulas for how many pieces of each size they have.

The dimension ladder

Every step up works the same way. Drag a point and it traces a line. Drag the line sideways and it sweeps a square. Drag the square at right angles to itself and you get a cube. Drag the cube in a fourth direction, at right angles to all three, and you get a tesseract.

The counts follow a pattern you can predict: corners double each time (1, 2, 4, 8, 16), and edges go 0, 1, 4, 12, 32, because each step copies the old edges and adds one new edge per corner. Nothing about the 4th step is special: you just can't point in that direction.

Flatland: how a slice shows the 4th dimension

Imagine flat creatures living in a plane. If a ball passed through their world, they'd never see a ball: just a dot that grows into a circle, then shrinks and vanishes. That's all a 2D being can see of a 3D object: one slice at a time.

Slice mode does this one level up. A 3D knife slides through a still 4D shape, and you see the part the knife is passing through. A hypersphere shows up as a ball that grows and shrinks. A tesseract fed in corner first shows up as a tetrahedron that swells into an octahedron and back.

Why there are only six regular 4D shapes

In 3D, the faces around each corner have to add up to less than 360°, or they lie flat. Triangles fit 3, 4 or 5 around a corner, squares 3 and pentagons 3. That's why there are exactly five Platonic solids.

In 4D the same idea moves up a level: the cells around each edge must leave a gap. The angle between two faces of a tetrahedron is about 70.5°, so 3, 4 or 5 fit around an edge: the 5-cell, the 16-cell and the 600-cell. Cubes (90°) fit 3: the tesseract. Octahedra (109.5°) fit 3: the 24-cell. Dodecahedra (116.6°) fit 3: the 120-cell. Icosahedra (138.2°) can't, because 3 of them already pass 360°. That makes six.

In 5D and above, the angles get too tight for everything except three shapes: the simplex, the hypercube and the cross-polytope. 4D is the richest dimension of all.

Duals: swapping corners and cells

Put a corner in the middle of every cell of a shape, join corners whose cells touch, and you get its dual. The cube and octahedron are duals in 3D. In 4D, the tesseract (16 corners, 8 cells) pairs with the 16-cell (8 corners, 16 cells), and the 120-cell pairs with the 600-cell. The 5-cell and the 24-cell are their own duals.

The morph slides a shape all the way to its dual, through every halfway shape in between.

Reading the names: truncated, rectified, and the rest

Most shapes here are a regular shape with pieces cut away:

Truncated: each corner sliced off. Rectified: cut so deep the cuts meet at the middle of every edge. Bitruncated: halfway between a shape and its dual. Cantellated: the edges shaved down too. Runcinated: the cells pushed apart and the gaps filled. Omnitruncated: every corner, edge and face cut at once.

All of these come from one trick, Wythoff's construction. Set up four mirrors, put a point between them, and let the mirrors copy it. Where the point sits decides which shape you get. That's exactly how the morph works.

Schläfli symbols: {4,3,3} and friends

A regular shape can be named with a short list of numbers. {4} is a square. {4,3} is a cube: squares, 3 around each corner. {4,3,3} is the tesseract: cubes, 3 around each edge. The 600-cell is {3,3,5}: tetrahedra, 5 around each edge.

Flip the list and you get the dual: {3,3,4} is the 16-cell, the tesseract's dual. A fraction like 5/2 means a pentagram, a star drawn without lifting your pen. That's how the star polytopes get names like {5/2,5,3}.

Turning in 4D: planes, not axes

In 3D we say things spin "around an axis", but really they turn in a plane: a spinning top turns in the horizontal plane, and the axis is just the one direction left over. In 4D there are two directions left over, so a 4D rotation has a whole fixed plane instead of an axis.

Stranger still, 4D has room for two separate planes at right angles to each other, XY and ZW, so a shape can turn in both at once. In a double rotation at equal speeds, nothing stays still at all: every point travels in a circle. 3D has no equivalent.

High dimensions and networks

Three families exist in every dimension, and they're three kinds of network: the hypercube (2ⁿ nodes, each linked to n others), the simplex (n+1 nodes, all linked) and the cross-polytope (2n nodes, linked to all but their opposite).

Ping a hypercube and watch the ring sizes: 1, n, then the rest of Pascal's triangle. Most corners sit at the middle distance, n/2 steps from the start, and that crowding in the middle gets more extreme as n grows. Distances spread too: in a 16D cube with edges 1 unit long, the corners are 2 units from the centre. And a ball fitted inside a 10D cube fills only about 0.25% of it, so nearly all the volume is out in the corners.

Machine learning works in spaces with hundreds or thousands of dimensions, where these effects are normal. It's part of the "curse of dimensionality".

Round shapes: the 3-sphere and the Hopf fibration

The 4D ball's surface, the 3-sphere, is a 3D space that curves back on itself: travel straight in any direction and you come back to where you started. It can be split into two solid doughnuts glued along a flat torus, the Clifford torus. That's the duocylinder's ridge.

It can also be filled completely with circles, no two touching, every one linked through every other. That's the Hopf fibration. It turns up in physics too, in the maths of spin and of light.

A short history

Ludwig Schläfli found all six regular 4D shapes around 1852. His work wasn't published until 1901, after his death. Charles Howard Hinton coined the word "tesseract" in 1888 and wrote popular books on picturing the 4th dimension. Edmund Hess completed the list of ten star polytopes in 1883. Alicia Boole Stott, with no formal training, worked out the 3D slices of the regular 4D shapes by hand and built cardboard models of them; she also brought the word "polytope" into English. In 1965, John Conway and Michael Guy used a computer to find the grand antiprism. In the 1970s, Thomas Banchoff made some of the first computer films of a tesseract turning in 4D.

Glossary

Cell: one of the 3D pieces that make up a 4D shape's surface, the way faces make up a polyhedron's. Facet: the biggest pieces of a shape's surface in any dimension (cells, in 4D). Vertex figure: the small shape you get by slicing off one corner. In 4D it's a polyhedron: a tetrahedron for the tesseract, an icosahedron for the 600-cell, a cube for the 24-cell. Projection: a lower-dimensional shadow. Slice or cross-section: where a hyperplane cuts through. Net: the cells unfolded and laid out flat in one dimension lower. Uniform: every corner looks the same and every cell is a regular or uniform shape. W: the name used here for the fourth direction.
